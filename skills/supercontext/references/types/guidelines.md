# Type: guidelines

Rules the assistant applies to its work: brand guidelines, editorial policy, compliance rules, what can and can't be promised, and style and voice. It is consulted while producing or reviewing work.

## What makes it usable
- Scope: which work the rules apply to (all public copy, only emails, only replies to clients).
- Each rule as an observable instruction, with its condition, its exception and, when it isn't obvious, its reason.
- Priority: what wins when two rules collide, or when a client's request contradicts a rule.
- For style and voice: the audience, the voice translated into concrete choices, preferred and avoided words, and poor and good pairs.

## Diagnosis questions
- Which work will be checked against these rules?
- Which rules are mandatory, which are preferences, and which are just habits? Only the first two go in.
- What goes wrong most often today? Show a real case.
- Are there exceptions by channel, client, product or situation?
- Who decides when two rules collide?
- For voice: is there an approved text that sounds right, and one that sounds wrong?

## Materials that help
The brand or style manual, the policy document, approved texts, rejected texts with the reason, and the corrections reviewers make most often.

## Typical skeleton
1. Scope: what the rules apply to, and what wins when they collide
2. Rules grouped by topic, each with condition, exception and reason
3. For voice: who we write for, the voice in concrete choices, words to use and avoid
4. Poor and good pairs for the rules that are hard to apply
5. Quick check: the rules most often broken, as a short list

## Rules for this type
- Write rules as instructions to apply, in the positive: "Write numbers up to ten in words" rather than "Avoid numerals".
- Translate every adjective into choices: "friendly" becomes second person, contractions, sentences under 20 words, no jargon.
- Give the reason in one short sentence when the rule isn't obvious; the model extends it to cases the rule didn't foresee.
- Keep the exception in the same bullet as the rule.
- Label each rule as mandatory or preferred when the material mixes both, so the model knows what it can bend.
- Show a poor and good pair, labeled as examples, for the rules a description doesn't make clear. Never let an example contradict a rule.
- Rules that apply to every task in the project (a disclaimer, a legal limit) also need a line in the project instructions; say so in the triage.

## Pitfalls
- Values and adjectives instead of rules ("be authentic", "premium tone").
- Long lists of prohibitions with no alternative ("never use…", "don't…").
- Rules from the manual that no one follows, or that contradict the approved texts; ask which one is true.
- Overriding emphasis (caps, "CRITICAL") that makes the model apply a rule where it doesn't fit.

## Sample entry for the plan
One rule as it will appear, with its condition, exception and reason, and a poor and good pair if the rule needs one.
