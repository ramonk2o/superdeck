# Case: research presentation

Decks that present research findings: quantitative (survey, behavioral data), qualitative (interviews, focus groups, ethnography), social listening, market research or desk research. The audience wants to **understand what was discovered and what it changes in their decisions**.

## What the audience needs to leave knowing
- The answer to the research question.
- The few insights that change a decision.
- How reliable the basis for these findings is.

## Diagnosis questions
- What was the research question, and which decision should it inform?
- What was the methodology? Sample size and profile, period, limitations?
- Which findings surprise or contradict what the audience believes?
- Which findings confirm what was already known (and need less space)?
- For each finding: what is the implication? (Finding + implication = insight.)
- Are there segments that behave very differently?
- Are there quotes or examples that bring the findings to life?

## How to phrase the central idea
Main insight + implication.
- Weak: "The research brought several learnings about the consumer."
- Strong: "People who pay in installments aren't short of money — they are organizing the month's budget; credit communication should talk about control, not urgency."

## Recommended arcs
- **Insights first** (default): answer → insights in order of importance → implications.
- **By research question**, when the audience commissioned specific questions and expects an answer to each.
- **Journey / persona**, when the findings are organized along the consumer's experience.

## Typical skeleton
1. The question and the answer in one sentence
2. Methodology on one slide (the detail goes to the appendix)
3–7. One insight per slide: finding + evidence + implication
8. Consolidated implications / recommendations
Appendix: questionnaire, sample, full data, more quotes.

## Pitfalls
- Following the questionnaire's order, question by question, instead of the order of importance.
- Data without interpretation: "43% said X" without saying what it means.
- Too many verbatim quotes — one or two well-chosen ones are worth more than ten.
- Extrapolating a small or qualitative sample as if it were representative.
- Hiding the research's limitations.
- Every finding with the same visual weight.

## Typical visuals
Sorted horizontal bars, quote with the interviewee's profile, segment heatmap, persona, journey map, comparison between segments, big number with context.

## Specific stress test
- Which insight would make someone change a decision tomorrow?
- Is this finding about what people say or about what they do?
- What can't the research claim, and does the audience know that?
