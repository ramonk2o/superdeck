# Case: strategy proposal

Decks that recommend a path: an annual plan, a new channel approach, an investment, a repositioning, a change of priority. The audience wants to understand **why change, why this path, and what they need to approve**.

## What the audience needs to leave knowing
- Which problem or opportunity justifies acting now.
- What the recommendation is, and why it beats the alternatives.
- What will be done, with what resources, and how we'll know it worked.
- Exactly what is being asked of them.

## Diagnosis questions
- What is the problem or opportunity, in one sentence? Why now?
- Who approves, and what does that person need to see to say yes?
- Which alternatives were considered, including "do nothing"? Why were they discarded?
- Which criteria define the best choice (cost, time, risk, impact)?
- What does this strategy stop doing? (Every strategy has trade-offs.)
- Which resources are needed: budget, people, time, technology?
- How will success be measured, and over what time frame?
- What are the main risks and how will they be mitigated?

## How to phrase the central idea
Recommendation + reason + ask.
- Weak: "We need to rethink our CRM strategy."
- Strong: "We should focus CRM on the 20% of customers who generate 70% of revenue, trading volume for relevance — we are asking for approval for a 3-month pilot."

## Recommended arcs
- **SCR** (default): shared situation → complication that forces a change → recommended resolution.
- **Answer first**, for executives short on time: recommendation → reasons → plan.
- **What is vs. what could be**, when the proposal needs to mobilize and not just convince.

## Typical skeleton
1. Recommendation and ask
2. Situation: the context everyone recognizes
3. Complication: what changed or what is at risk
4. Alternatives evaluated and criteria
5. Why the recommendation wins
6. What it looks like: the main workstreams
7. Resources, schedule and success metrics
8. Risks and mitigation
9. Next steps and the ask, again
Appendix: supporting analyses, cost breakdown, benchmarks.

## Pitfalls
- Tactics disguised as strategy: a list of actions with no central choice connecting them.
- Not showing the alternatives — the audience wonders "why not X?" and the deck doesn't answer.
- A strategy without trade-offs: if it doesn't say what it stops doing, it isn't a choice.
- A diagnosis that is too long, with the recommendation squeezed in at the end.
- A vague ask ("support", "alignment") instead of a concrete decision.
- No success metric or time horizon.

## Typical visuals
2x2 matrix of options, alternatives vs. criteria table, roadmap/timeline, workstream board, chart of the problem (the trend that justifies acting), risk table.

## Specific stress test
- Which alternative would an internal competitor defend, and why does it lose?
- What happens if nobody does anything in the next 6 months?
- What is the smallest version of this plan that still proves the thesis?
