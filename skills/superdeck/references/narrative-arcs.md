# Narrative arcs

Catalog of structures for step 1.5. Propose 2 or 3 that make sense for the audience and objective, with pros and cons, and recommend one.

## Answer first (pyramid)
**Structure:** conclusion/recommendation → reasons that support it → evidence for each reason.
**When to use:** executives and decision-makers short on time; decks sent for reading; when the audience already trusts the presenter.
**When to avoid:** when the conclusion will meet resistance and the audience needs to be led to it.
**Origin:** Barbara Minto's pyramid principle.

## SCR — Situation, Complication, Resolution
**Structure:** a situation everyone recognizes → a complication that changes the picture (problem, threat, opportunity) → the proposed resolution. SCQA variant: includes the question the complication raises.
**When to use:** proposals, recommendations, decks in which the audience needs to understand why to change.
**When to avoid:** when the situation is so well known that re-explaining it is tiring — in that case, shorten it or start with the complication.

## Problem → solution → benefit
**Structure:** a problem the audience feels → solution → what improves and for whom.
**When to use:** pitches, internal selling, proposals for a tool or process.
**When to avoid:** when the audience doesn't recognize the problem — it has to be proven first.

## Before / after / bridge
**Structure:** how it is today → how it could be → what takes you from one to the other.
**When to use:** concrete transformations, results of a change, pitches with visual proof.
**When to avoid:** when the "after" is abstract or has no evidence.

## What is vs. what could be
**Structure:** alternates between the current reality and the possible future, raising the contrast up to a call to action.
**When to use:** presentations meant to mobilize, vision, culture change, launches.
**When to avoid:** analytical or accountability decks.
**Origin:** Nancy Duarte, *Resonate*.

## Chronological journey
**Structure:** what happened, in order.
**When to use:** retrospectives, post-mortems, history needed to understand a decision, customer journey.
**When to avoid:** almost always as the main structure of a results analysis — chronology is rarely the order of importance.

## Guiding questions
**Structure:** each section answers a question the audience has ("Did it work?", "Why?", "What now?").
**When to use:** commissioned research with defined questions, audiences that arrive with clear doubts.
**When to avoid:** when the questions don't follow an order of importance.

## Storyline rules (apply to any arc)
- **Assertion titles:** each title states the slide's conclusion, as a full sentence.
- **One slide, one idea.** If the title needs an "and", it is probably two slides.
- **Horizontal reading:** the titles read in sequence tell the whole story.
- **Vertical reading:** the body of each slide proves its title — nothing more, nothing less.
- **Strong start and end:** the first content slide says where we're going; the last says what to do.
