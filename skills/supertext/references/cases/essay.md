# Case: essay

An argument or a reflection on a subject, short or long. The reader wants to know **what the author thinks, why, and why it matters to them**.

## What the reader needs to leave with
- The author's position, in one sentence they could repeat or argue with.
- The reason that moves them most, with its evidence: an argument, not a survey.
- Why it matters to them: what they would see or do differently.
- The strongest objection, and why the author still holds the position.

## Materials that help
The notes or draft; the sources the argument rests on; one or two texts of the author's they like (own voice), or a passage of the named author.

## Diagnosis questions
- What do you believe about this that most of your readers don't?
- What happened to you that made you think so?
- What would a smart reader who disagrees say, and what do you answer?
- Which part of your view are you least sure of? It decides where the text hedges.
- Are you arguing (to convince) or reflecting (to explore)?
- Where will it run, and does the reader know you?

## How to phrase the central idea
Position + reason + what follows. The position is the author's, as "Propose before asking" in `SKILL.md` says.
- Weak: "Remote work has pros and cons."
- Strong: "Remote work costs juniors most, because they lose the overheard conversations that used to train them; teams that go remote have to schedule what the office did by chance."

## Typical structures
- **Short (up to about 1,500 words):** the position early → one main reason with a concrete case → the objection and the answer → what follows for the reader. No headings.
- **Long:** an opening case or problem → the position → two to four reasons, one section each, with evidence → the strongest objection given its full weight → implications → a close that returns to the opening with something changed. Headings optional.
- **Reflection:** a question → attempts at an answer and why each falls short → where the author lands, with honest uncertainty.

## Pitfalls
- "On one hand, on the other" with no position.
- The thesis arriving in the last paragraph of a short essay.
- A straw-man objection, or none.
- Research crowding out the author: citations in place of the argument.
- Abstractions with no case.
- A close that adds nothing the middle didn't give.

## Sample passage for the plan
The opening, 100 to 200 words: it shows the voice and how fast the position arrives. In a long essay whose risk is the objection, the paragraph that answers it.
