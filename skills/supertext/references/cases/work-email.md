# Case: work email

A work email that informs or asks for something. It is read between other tasks, often only the first lines. The reader wants to know **what is being asked of them, by when, and what they need to decide it**.

## What the reader needs to leave with
- From the subject and the first two lines: what changed or what is asked, and by when.
- What they must do or decide, and just enough context to do it; detail goes in an attachment or an offer to talk.
- For bad news: the new fact, its cause in one line, what is being done about it, and what it means for them.

## Materials that help
The thread being answered; the facts (dates, numbers, names); a past email to this reader whose tone worked.

## Diagnosis questions
- What do you need from them, and by when? If nothing, what must they know?
- Who are they to you (client, boss, peer), and how will they take this?
- For bad news: the new date or fact, the cause in one line, what you're doing, what you offer.
- Who is in copy, and does that change anything?
- Is there anything you can't say (a commitment, blame, internal detail)?

## How to phrase the central idea
The news or the ask, with its deadline, carried by the subject line.
- Weak: subject "Project update"; opening "I hope this finds you well. I wanted to give you an update."
- Strong: subject "Launch moves to March 14 — approval needed by Friday"; opening "The launch moves from February 28 to March 14. The payment partner's certification failed last week, and we need two weeks to fix and retest."

## Typical structures
- **Ask:** the ask and deadline → why, in one or two lines → what they need to decide → an offer of a call.
- **Bad news:** the news → the cause, owned, in one line → what you're doing → what it means for them, or what you offer → the next contact.
- **Update:** the change that matters → status in short lines → what they must do, or "nothing needed".
- **Format:** plain text, no headings, short paragraphs, one ask per email, about 150 words when possible.

## Pitfalls
- The ask in the last paragraph.
- Over-apologizing, stacked hedges, or blaming third parties the reader didn't ask about.
- Several asks: the reader answers one.
- "Soon" or "next week" where a date exists.
- A thread subject that no longer matches what the email asks.

## Sample passage for the plan
A short email follows "A text of a few lines" in `SKILL.md`. For a longer email, the sample is the first paragraph, the one that carries the ask.
