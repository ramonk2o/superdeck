# Style

Used in steps 0.4 and 1.5. A style is settled as observable traits the user can check on the sample passage, never as adjectives alone ("elegant", "light"), because adjectives mean different things to the user and to the model.

## Sources of a style
- **A register:** formal, casual, technical, conversational. Derive the traits from the reader and the occasion.
- **The user's own voice:** read one or two texts they wrote and like, and describe what they actually do, not what they say they do.
- **A named author or book:** see "Writing in the style of an author" below.

## Traits to settle
Four to seven traits, each written as "does X, not Y" so it can be checked on the sample. Pick the ones that make this style different from a neutral text; skip the rest.
1. **Sentence length and rhythm** — for example, "mostly 8 to 15 words; one long sentence to land a point".
2. **Paragraphs** — length, how they open, whether one-sentence paragraphs are allowed.
3. **Person and distance** — I, we, you or impersonal; whether the text addresses the reader.
4. **Vocabulary** — plain or technical, the jargon allowed, contractions.
5. **Figures and examples** — how often metaphors appear and where they come from; a concrete example per claim or not; humor or irony.
6. **How the argument moves** — states the position early or builds to it; how it concedes; how certain claims sound.
7. **Never does** — two to four explicit bans.

## Writing in the style of an author
- Describe the author as traits, stated as your reading ("my reading of X: …"), and mark the ones you are unsure of. Your idea of an author can be thin or wrong, and the user may not know the author well enough to judge it; a passage the user likes grounds the traits in something both of you can see.
- Imitate traits, never passages: write new sentences that share the author's rhythm, distance and choices, and don't reproduce or closely paraphrase the author's text.
- Leave out signature phrases, catchphrases and recurring images; they turn the text into a caricature.
- Say which traits carry over to the language of the text and which don't, when it differs from the author's.
- Never present the text as the author's.

## Pitfalls
- Adjectives standing in for traits ("fluid", "engaging").
- Pastiche: every sentence showing every trait at once.
- The model's own habits slipping in as if they were part of the style.
- A style that works against what the reader needs. When they conflict, the reader wins.
